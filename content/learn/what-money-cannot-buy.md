The instinctive response to a shortage is that someone should invest in fixing
it. It is usually wrong, and understanding why is one of the more useful things
this site can offer.

## The transformer case

Large power transformers have lead times measured in years. They gate how fast
data centres can be connected to the grid, which makes them one of the most
consequential constraints in technology right now.

Money has been available for two years. The queue has not shortened.

That is not a failure of willingness. It is what happens when the binding step
is qualified winding capacity, specialised core steel from a handful of mills,
and the fact that nearly every unit is custom-designed for its site. A cheque
does not shorten any of those, and the fact that capital arrived and nothing
changed is *evidence about where the constraint is*.

## Why the kind of money matters more than the amount

Capital is not one thing. It comes with a mandate and a clock, and those decide
what it can build.

Venture capital needs a small number of enormous outcomes, so it cannot fund a
factory that earns a steady margin on a known product — however badly the world
needs that factory. Project finance is the largest pool available to heavy
industry and it will not move without a contracted buyer. Public markets are
patient only as long as each holder individually is, which is not the same
thing as patient. A grant can pay for something that will never return its cost
and can also be withdrawn when a government changes.

So the question is never "is there money?" It is "is there money whose mandate
allows this, on a clock that survives the build?" For a fifteen-year asset, the
list of candidates is short: sovereign funds, development banks, export credit
agencies, and corporate balance sheets willing to take a strategic loss.

## When money genuinely does help

Sometimes it does, and the section says so. Superconducting tape for fusion
magnets is made by young companies on small production lines. There, capital
really does add capacity — which is why fusion programmes have been buying
stakes in their own suppliers rather than merely placing orders.

That is the shape to look for: a young industry, small lines, and demand that
is credible enough to underwrite expansion. It is rarer than it sounds.

## The uncomfortable version

For most rows on this site, the honest answer to "would more money fix this?"
is no. Sometimes the answer is worse than no: silicon carbide capacity was
built ahead of demand that did not arrive, and more money would make the
oversupply deeper.

None of this is advice about where to put money, and this site does not give
any. It is a map of who can fund what, and of the rather large number of
problems that funding does not solve.
