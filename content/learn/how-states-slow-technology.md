When people imagine a government slowing a technology, they imagine a ban.
Bans are rare, loud and easy to route around. The instruments that actually
change what gets built are quieter, and three of them do most of the work.

## Export controls

A rule that something may not leave the country without permission. The
permission is the point: a licence regime is not a ban, it is a tap, and it can
be opened or closed without passing a new law.

What makes them powerful is *where* they are placed. A control on a finished
product is easy to design around. A control on one purification step, or on the
machine that performs it, catches everything downstream — including products
made in other countries by other companies, if the rule reaches that far. That
is why the list of controlled items so often reads like industrial trivia.

The signal to watch is scope creep at the tool layer. A control on a material
is a price event. A control on the equipment that makes the material is a
capacity event, and those last years.

## Trade defence

Tariffs, anti-dumping duties, safeguards. These protect domestic producers from
imports, and they can help or hurt the thing you care about depending on
whether the domestic producer actually exists at the grade you need.

This is where the most instructive failures happen. A measure written to
support domestic polysilicon drove away both customers of the only sizeable
domestic plant, which is now at risk of closing. The intent was to loosen a
bottleneck; the effect so far has been to tighten it. That is not an argument
against trade measures. It is an argument for checking which grade of a product
a measure covers, because "polysilicon" is two different products and only one
of them goes into chips.

## Permission to build

The least discussed and often the most binding. A grid connection queue, an
environmental permit, a reactor licence. Nothing is forbidden; it simply takes
years, and there is nothing to buy to make it faster.

The interesting thing about this category is that it is the one where reform
demonstrably works. When a regulator changes how a queue is processed — first
ready, first served, with deadlines — the queue can move. That is why this site
records process reform as an instrument alongside export controls, and why one
of its published predictions is about whether a specific reform will work.

## How to read the Policy section

Every instrument carries what it does, to which bottleneck, on what date, and
one sentence from its own source. A status that is not simply "in force" has to
explain itself: adopted but not yet in force, under investigation, suspended.

And where a named organisation publicly asked for a rule, it is recorded — but
only where that organisation says so in its own document. Inferring who lobbied
for what from who benefits is how a research project turns into a conspiracy
board, so this site does not do it. Exactly one instrument currently has a
named backer. The rest say so plainly.
